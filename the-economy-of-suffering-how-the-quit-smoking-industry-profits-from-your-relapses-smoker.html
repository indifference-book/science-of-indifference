The Economy of Suffering: How the Quit-Smoking Industry Profits From Your Relapses, Smoker

Published April 24, 2026 · 5 min read

All established smoking cessation programs rest on a single premise: the smoker must endure and comply. Worse, some openly destructive works treat a relapse like a moral crime—on par with theft or tax evasion. Yet science and basic learning principles don’t punish students for making mistakes.

So the question arises: who benefits from this mindset? Let’s look at the examples.

Consider the response from one such “specialist” who makes quitting his profession. Published in 2002 on WhyQuit.com in an article titled “We Understand Why You Relapsed,” Joel Spitzer boldly positions himself as an enforcer of some kind of law in a field that collapses under basic facts.

“The person broke the law of addiction, took a puff of nicotine, and pays the mandatory penalty—a relapse,” he states without hesitation.

From there, the logic branches into a series of contradictions:

- promises to “understand the reasons for the relapse,” then immediately dismisses all explanations as “complete nonsense” and forbids seeking justification;

- compares a single puff to jumping off a roof, demanding that the mistake be treated as a final verdict rather than a point for correction;

- claims support is dangerous because “people on the ledge should hear not sympathy, but a warning of consequences,” while simultaneously using fear as his only retention tool;

- labels communities that treat relapse calmly as “too lenient on excuses,” ignoring the fact that craving cycles are documented in data, not invented;

- demands an absolute result—“never take another puff”—despite addiction, by definition, having waves and troughs;

- insists strict policies are needed to “save lives,” yet sidelines those who can’t withstand the pressure, recommending they leave for other platforms;

- steps into the role of an enforcer who doesn’t study the mechanics of failure, but merely logs violations and delivers verdicts.

Twenty years later, on the same site, John R. Polito publishes “No Legitimate Excuse for Relapse.” This time, the author descends into outright distortion:

- moralizing that frames relapse as a “law violation” and “self-poisoning,” despite science recording entirely different data;

- absurd analogies (hard drugs, building ledges) that illustrate elementary substitutions aimed at triggering emotions whose sole purpose is to intimidate;

- attempts to provoke shame, which in itself is a cowardly manipulation.

Meanwhile, the author completely ignores real human triggers—habits, rituals, needs, and coping skills. Instead, he forces the unfortunate smoker into an “all or nothing” mindset. Most distressingly, he uses pseudo-scientific terms as if they were common knowledge.

If you dig into other programs—even the most empathetic ones—their core message still revolves around rigid compliance: the smoker “must” and “must endure.”

In contrast, 2016 research from Toronto tracking thousands of smokers shows that the average number of attempts before success is counted in the dozens. International ITC surveys confirm that failed attempts aren’t exceptions—they’re the norm. This isn’t a “lack of willpower.” It’s the neurobiology of habit rewiring: neural pathways aren’t overwritten with a snap; they weaken through repetition and calibration.

These findings show that smoking is simply a habit. A complex one, driven by biochemistry and deeply embedded social scripts—but a habit nonetheless. Strangely, no one expects you to master riding a bike or playing the accordion perfectly on your first try, yet that’s exactly what many quitting programs demand.

Even Allen Carr’s famous book doesn’t address relapse at all. To him, it’s an unnatural anomaly—not worth describing.

So why is the nicotine cessation industry so fixated on rigid demands and strict compliance?

The answer is as cynical as the rest of today’s system: it’s financially profitable. It keeps groups and courses going. It benefits everyone—except the smoker.

If smokers stopped stressing—and even learned to enjoy the quitting process, as described in The Science of Indifference—the profits of these “gurus” would drop significantly, very mildly put.

It’s a subtle psychological maneuver: forcing smokers to follow complex scripts that contradict basic skill acquisition. When every “authoritative” source demands something that defies simple logic.

The mechanism is simple: a program or course is built on zero tolerance for relapse. Yet relapse happens—it’s statistically inevitable. That triggers a need for “recovery,” “support,” or a “new plan.” Which, in turn, creates demand for subscriptions, upgraded courses, or books. Another session is booked, membership is renewed. The cycle isn’t closed by quitting; it’s closed by re-engagement. NRT, tracking apps, coach-enforcers, “strict-rule” groups—they’re all built into the same economy. Relapse isn’t treated as a calibration point; it’s treated as a monetization opportunity.

Rigid frameworks and “never smoke again” mantras trigger the Abstinence Violation Effect. Where even one puff is perceived as a system failure, and guilt paralyzes the prefrontal cortex. As is well known, this is the brain’s executive control center, responsible for:

- decision-making and evaluating consequences;

- critical and abstract thinking;

- impulse inhibition;

- working memory usage and goal retention;

- emotional regulation.

Under shame, stress, and guilt, the brain simply reverts to the old script without resistance. It’s basic neurology.

Tension, control, and self-condemnation drain self-regulation resources, turning quitting into an endurance marathon. And an exhaustion marathon is a predictably renewable cycle that becomes not a skill-building exercise, but a torment.

And it’s done with such force that no other perspective is allowed. While those other perspectives represent the natural way habits change.

In other words, if you listen to these mantras—get your wallet ready.
